🇰🇷 한국어

Second, compare GST with Hirschi's Social Bond Theory. Hirschi argued that weak social bonds release people to offend — when attachment, commitment, involvement, and belief are low, there is nothing holding the individual back from crime. Agnew, on the other hand, argued that strain pushes people toward crime. We can characterize this as a release model versus a pressure model.

Third, consider Social Learning Theory as developed by Ronald Akers. Both GST and social learning theory recognize the importance of peer influence — delinquent peers can model criminal behavior and reinforce it. However, the theoretical emphasis differs: social learning theory highlights reinforcement, imitation, and differential definitions, while GST emphasizes the emotional response to strain as the proximate cause of offending.

Fourth, there is an interesting connection between GST and Labeling Theory. Being formally labeled as a criminal or deviant can itself constitute a strain — it is, in Agnew's terms, a form of the presentation of negatively valued stimuli. Thus GST can incorporate labeling effects: the stigma of a criminal label generates anger and frustration, which may in turn increase the likelihood of further offending.

A meta-analysis by Landenberger and Lipsey (2005) found that CBT programs produced an average twenty-five percent reduction in recidivism across multiple studies.

To summarize, empirical research from Agnew and White, Broidy, Mazerolle, and cross-cultural studies like Moon and colleagues' work in South Korea all provide support for the core mechanisms of GST. Comparisons with Merton, social bond theory, social learning theory, and labeling theory reveal GST's unique contribution: it places emotion — especially anger — at the heart of the causal process. And policy applications such as CBT, bullying prevention, and restorative justice demonstrate that GST is not merely an abstract academic exercise but a theory with real-world consequences for reducing crime.
