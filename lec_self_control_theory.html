🇰🇷 한국어

⚖️ Theory Comparisons
S32

How does Self-Control Theory compare with other major theories? Let us start with Hirschi's own Social Bond Theory from 1969. In that earlier work, Hirschi stressed external social bonds — attachment, commitment, involvement, and belief — as the key restraints on criminal behavior. The shift to self-control raises a critical question: does the internal trait of self-control simply subsume those external bonds?

Next, consider Robert Agnew's General Strain Theory. Agnew argues that crime results from negative emotions produced by strain — such as anger and frustration caused by blocked goals. Gottfredson and Hirschi would respond that people with low self-control are simply more likely to experience strain and commit crime — making self-control a confounding variable rather than strain being the true cause.

Finally, the deepest theoretical clash is with life-course criminology. Robert Sampson and John Laub argue that change is possible through turning points — events like marriage, stable employment, or military service that redirect a person's trajectory. Gottfredson and Hirschi insist that self-control is stable. This debate — stability versus change — is arguably the biggest controversy in modern criminology.

🏫 Policy Applications — Real Programs
S36

If self-control is truly formed in early childhood, what can we actually do about it? Several real-world programs have demonstrated success. The most famous is the Perry Preschool Project. Schweinhart and colleagues conducted a 40-year follow-up of disadvantaged children in Ypsilanti, Michigan. By age 40, the intervention group had 46 percent fewer arrests than the control group.
